A duck with four legs

Bozeat, Lambon Ralph, Graham, Patterson, Wilkin, Rowland, Rogers & Hodges (2003): investigating the structure of conceptual knowledge using picture drawing in semantic dementia.

Dear Sezen,

This paper answers a deceptively simple question: when meaning is slowly destroyed by disease, what goes first? The answer tells us how meaning was built in the first place. Patients with semantic dementia can still see, copy and reason, yet they gradually forget what a duck is. Ask them to draw one from its name and they leave out the bill and the webbed feet — and sometimes give it four legs. That error is not random. It is a window into how the brain stores concepts: as overlapping features, in which what many things share is sturdy and what makes a thing special is fragile.

Why should you care beyond the exam? First, this is the logic of neuropsychology: learn about the healthy mind from a selectively damaged one. Second, the paper is a masterclass in looking at the pattern of errors, not just the score — a habit that serves you just as well when you read assessment or engagement data at NN. And third, semantic dementia is a reminder for inclusive workplaces that fluent speech can hide a real loss of word meaning. (Those last two links are my applications, not claims from the paper.)

🗺️ The lessons

🧵 Synthesis — one thesis for the whole paper

Thesis: semantic dementia does not erase knowledge at random — it strips concepts from the outside in, and that pattern reveals a single, distributed, feature-based semantic system in which shared features are robust and distinctive features are fragile. Because SD damages meaning while sparing perception, praxis and short-term visual memory, drawing becomes a clean window on conceptual knowledge. Study 1 shows that drawing, naming and word–picture matching decline together (r = .96–.99), so one central store is failing, and that drawings lose their distinctive details: artefacts become boxes, animals become generic, sometimes with four legs. Study 2 nails down the mechanism. Performance worsens precisely as a task leans more on meaning (immediate copy > delayed copy > drawing to name) and as the disease advances, while meaningless shapes are copied normally. Distinctive features are omitted most, and the features that intrude are those shared across a whole domain. Even the apparent “animal” deficit dissolves once feature distinctiveness is taken into account: animals simply have more shared features to intrude. The hierarchy seen in SD — duck → bird → animal — therefore need not be built into memory. It emerges from how features overlap, the same overlap that makes some members typical and others not.

Lecture 1 of 11 · Introduction · What is semantic dementia?

🧩Semantic dementia: a natural experiment

When one system breaks and the others keep working, the damage shows you how that system was built.

  • 📖Semantic memory = our knowledge about objects, facts and concepts, as well as words and their meaning.
  • 🧩Semantic dementia: a very selective, progressive impairment of conceptual knowledge, with circumscribed temporal-lobe atrophy.
  • 🗣️Patients present with a progressive loss of vocabulary in both expressive and receptive language, in the context of fluent speech.
  • 🌐Previous work: deficits from sound recognition to naming-from-definition, with strong item consistency across modalities → a central, modality-independent deficit (Bozeat et al., 2000).
  • 🔬Other diseases (herpes simplex encephalitis, Alzheimer’s) also reveal semantic organisation, but SD’s selectivity makes it especially clean.
🪝 Memory hook: SD is like a library where only the dictionary is fading — the lights, shelves and librarians all still work, so every mistake tells you something about the dictionary.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “Semantic dementia is a disease that slowly wipes out what words and things mean, because part of the temporal lobe shrinks. People still talk fluently and can copy pictures, so if they make errors on meaning tasks, we know it’s their knowledge that’s broken — which lets us study how knowledge is organised.”
Because semantic dementia damages conceptual knowledge while sparing perception, praxis and reasoning, the pattern of its errors reveals the structure of the semantic system itself.

🧩 Comprehension Check

1. Define semantic dementia, including its neural basis.

A progressive neurodegenerative syndrome with a selective loss of conceptual knowledge across modalities, presenting as loss of vocabulary with fluent speech, caused by focal atrophy of the polar and inferolateral temporal lobes (usually left > right).

2. Why is SD a “particularly informative testing ground”?

The deficit is highly selective: semantic knowledge is impaired while perception, visuospatial skills, reasoning and working memory are relatively intact, so error patterns can be attributed to the semantic system itself.

Lecture 2 of 11 · Introduction · What SD can reveal

🌳Hierarchy or features?

Is knowledge stored as a tree of categories — or does the tree grow out of overlapping features?

  • 🌳Hierarchical view (Warrington, 1975): superordinate (living vs artefact), basic (animals, tools) and exemplar levels are represented separately; exemplars degrade first.
  • ⚠️But categories are messier: some members are more typical (Rosch, 1973), membership can be unclear, and some categories are more coherent (vehicles vs household items).
  • ☁️Feature view: concepts are sets of fine-grained attributes (from feature-listing studies); domains and categories emerge from similarity in feature structure.
  • 🎯Typicality falls out naturally: Garrard et al. (2001) — rated prototypicality is predicted by an exemplar’s similarity to the centroid (average) of its category.
  • 🤖Rogers et al. (2002a): damage to a feature-only network reproduces SD’s hierarchical errors.
🪝 Memory hook: the hierarchy is like the shape of a crowd seen from a plane — it looks organised into groups, but up close it is just individuals standing near people who resemble them.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “One idea is that the brain files knowledge in a tree: animal, then bird, then duck. The other is that it just stores features like ‘has wings’ and ‘has a bill’, and ducks and swans end up grouped because they share so many. The cool part is that a feature-only computer model, when damaged, loses knowledge in the same tree-like order as patients.”
The specific-to-general pattern of loss in SD need not mean memory is built as a hierarchy: the hierarchy can emerge from overlapping features.

🧩 Comprehension Check

1. What SD evidence seems to support a hierarchical organisation?

Longitudinal naming errors (duck → swan → bird → animal; Hodges et al., 1995) and sorting tasks, where assigning items to superordinate categories outlasts finer distinctions (Hodges et al., 1994).

2. Name three observations that call for a more complex view than a simple hierarchy.

Some concepts are more typical than others (Rosch, 1973); membership can be unclear or overlapping; some categories are far more coherent than others (vehicles vs household items).

3. How did Rogers et al. (2002a) reconcile the two views?

A connectionist network trained only on features reproduced the hierarchical pattern of loss when damaged, showing the hierarchy can emerge without being explicitly coded.

Lecture 3 of 11 · Introduction + Study 2 method · Feature distinctiveness

🎨Shared vs distinctive features

A duck’s wings tell you it is a bird; its green head feathers tell you it is a mallard. Which do you lose first?

  • 📏Features vary on a shared ↔ distinctive continuum: the green feathers of a male mallard are specific; a duck’s wings are true of most birds.
  • 🧮Score = proportion of items in the category that include the feature. Wings drawn on 6 of 8 birds → 0.75.
  • 🔀The same feature can be shared in one category and distinctive in another: “neck” is shared by birds and land animals, but distinctive among artefacts (only guitar and watering can).
  • 📊The distribution is bimodal — many very shared and many very distinctive features, few in between — so a 0.5 cut-off is sensible.
  • 🐾Living things have more shared features (0.59 vs 0.29 per item) — the key to explaining domain differences later.
🪝 Memory hook: shared features are the family surname (everyone has it); distinctive features are the first name (only you have it). Dementia forgets first names before surnames.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “Some features belong to lots of things — nearly every animal has eyes and legs. Others belong to one thing, like a camel’s hump. The researchers measured, for each feature, how many items in the category have it, and animals turned out to have far more shared features than man-made objects.”
Every feature can be scored by how many members of its category share it — and living things have many more shared features than artefacts.

📊 Tables & studies

StepHow distinctiveness was calculated (Study 2, following Garrard et al., 2001)
1Take every feature in every control drawing
2Score = proportion of items in the same semantic category that also include it (e.g., wings on 6/8 birds = 0.75), per control and per category
3Average across control participants
4Classify: shared-by-domain (> half of items in all categories of the domain), shared-by-category (> half in an intermediate category only), or distinctive (the rest)
5Result: animals and birds 0.59 shared per item; artefacts 0.29 (cf. Rogers et al., 2002a)

🧩 Comprehension Check

1. A control drew a neck on 7 of 8 land animals but on only 2 of 24 artefacts. How is “neck” classified?

Its score depends on the category: shared among animals (and birds), but distinctive among artefacts — exactly the paper’s point that distinctiveness is category-relative.

2. Why does the difference in shared features between domains matter?

If shared features survive damage and intrude, domains with more shared features (living things: 0.59 vs 0.29) will show more prototypical drawings and more intrusions — without needing a separate “living things” store.

Lecture 4 of 11 · Introduction · Using drawing as an assessment

✏️Drawing as a test of meaning

A drawing is a non-verbal feature list — if you score it by content rather than by beauty.

  • ✏️Drawing is widely used clinically (constructional apraxia, neglect, MMSE) and can be a rich, sensitive test of conceptual knowledge.
  • 🤐Unlike feature listing or definitions, it avoids the problem of anomia — patients don’t need words to show what they know.
  • ⚠️Drawbacks: different premorbid drawing skills, and scoring — “how good is this?” ratings are confounded by skill and ignore content.
  • 📋Solution: build a feature list from control drawings; a feature enters the scheme if ≥ 6 of 8 controls drew it; score = proportion present.
  • 🚫Items excluded: plug, barrel and alligator — controls misheard or misunderstood the names.
🪝 Memory hook: feature scoring is a checklist, not a beauty contest — a clumsy duck with a bill, wings and webbed feet beats a lovely duck without them.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “Healthy people drew each object and the researchers listed the parts most of them included — for a duck: body, head, two legs, webbed feet, beak, wings. Then they counted how many of those parts each patient drew. That measures knowledge, not talent.”
A drawing scored by the features healthy people consistently include is an objective, non-verbal measure of what a patient knows about an object.

Lecture 5 of 11 · Study 1 · Method

🧪Study 1: who, what and how

Before reading any result, know exactly who was tested, on what, and why each test is there.

  • 🧠Six SD patients seen by a neurologist, psychiatrist and neuropsychologist; depression, schizophrenia and treatable causes excluded; all met SD criteria.
  • 📏They span the whole range of severity: from mildly affected DM to severely impaired FM (MMSE 29 → 8).
  • 🧩The general battery checks what should be spared: reasoning (Raven’s), visuospatial skill and episodic memory (Rey figure), perception (VOSP).
  • 🔗The semantic battery uses the same 64 items across tasks, so the same concept can be compared in naming, matching and drawing.
  • 🔄Drawing: living and non-living items alternated and a fresh sheet each time, to minimise perseveration (repeating the previous drawing).
🪝 Memory hook: the same 64 items appear in every task — like giving each concept a passport that gets stamped in naming, matching and drawing.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “They tested six patients, from mild to severe, plus eight healthy people of similar age. Everyone did the same 64 objects in lots of tasks — naming, matching, drawing — plus tests of skills that should still be fine, like copying a complex figure.”
Using the same 64 items across every task lets the researchers ask whether the same concept fails in the same way whatever the task — the test of a single central store.

Lecture 6 of 11 · Study 1 · Results

📈Study 1: the numbers

Meaning collapses while copying stays near normal — and drawing falls in lock-step with naming.

  • 📉Patients drew far fewer target features: mean 0.53 (SD 0.31) vs 0.95 (SD 0.04) for controls; U = 1.5, p < .01.
  • 🔗Drawing correlated with naming and word–picture matching at r = .96–.99 (all p < .001) → one common cause.
  • 🧱Meanwhile Rey copy and Raven’s stay relatively preserved until very late → the impairment is selective.
  • 📊Regression: a trend for better drawings of familiar items (t = 1.78, p = .07); only KH showed a domain effect (man-made better, t = 5.4, p < .001).
  • 📝DM was mild at this stage (impaired only on Pyramids & Palm Trees), but declined on all semantic tests at follow-up; his daily vocabulary practice may have helped (footnote 1).
🪝 Memory hook: three measures falling together like three candles on one wick — when one dims, they all dim, because they burn the same fuel: conceptual knowledge.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “Patients included about half of the key features healthy people drew. And the worse someone was at naming, the worse they drew — almost perfectly in step. But copying a complicated figure stayed near normal, so it’s their knowledge, not their hand or eye.”
Drawing, naming and comprehension decline together (r = .96–.99) while copying is spared — so poor drawing reflects impoverished conceptual representations.

All three depend on the same thing — the integrity of central conceptual knowledge — so impairment in each arises from selective damage to that common semantic system.

Lecture 7 of 11 · Study 1 · Qualitative observations & discussion

📦Box-like lamps, prototypical ducks

Two domains, two kinds of error — and one explanation.

  • 📦Artefacts: distinctive features lost; drawings become simpler and box-like; incorrect features very rarely intrude.
  • 🦆Living things: distinctive features lost and a pull toward a familiar, “prototypical” animal, with wrong features added — four legs on a duck.
  • 🎯Misdrawings are semantically related (kangaroo → giraffe); cross-domain errors are extremely rare.
  • 🔁This mirrors the naming errors of SD (Hodges et al., 1995) and may reflect differences in feature distribution between domains (McRae & Cree, 2002; Rogers et al., 2002a).
  • ➡️Two limits motivate Study 2: the scheme couldn’t count intrusions, and drawing to name depends on verbal input.
🪝 Memory hook: when the details fade, a lamp melts into a box and a duck melts into a generic animal — each concept slides toward the average of its domain.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “When patients drew man-made things, the special parts disappeared and the objects turned into simple boxes. When they drew animals, the special parts disappeared too, but typical animal parts crept in — like a duck with four legs. Animals share lots of features, so there’s more ‘average animal’ to leak in.”
Distinctive features are lost in both domains; in the living domain, widely shared features also intrude, making drawings more prototypical.

🧩 Comprehension Check

1. How did errors differ between artefacts and living things in Study 1?

Both lost distinctive features; artefacts became box-like with very rare intrusions, whereas living things became more prototypical and gained incorrect shared features (e.g., four legs on a duck).

2. What explanation do the authors propose for this domain difference?

Shared features are more likely to be preserved and to intrude, and living things have more shared features — so living-thing drawings drift toward the prototype and show more intrusions.

Lecture 8 of 11 · Study 2 · Method

🕹️Study 2: isolating meaning

Vary how much a task needs meaning — and add a task that needs none — to pin the problem on semantics.

  • 👥Three new SD patients — DS (mild, 69), DC (moderate, 77), IF (severe, 66) — and 4 matched controls.
  • 🕹️Three conditions on separate occasions: immediate copy, delayed copy (≈10 s filled delay) and drawing to name.
  • ⏱️The 10 s delay came from pilot testing with DS: after just 10 s, his poor conceptual knowledge already affected his copies.
  • 💬If a name meant nothing, a non-perceptual prompt was given (“a bird that swims on ponds, waddles on land and lays eggs”) — never “it has a beak”.
  • 🔺Control task: delayed copying of geometric shapes (3–8 features) — almost no semantic content, so they test visual episodic memory alone.
🪝 Memory hook: the three conditions are a dimmer switch for meaning — immediate copy (lights off), delayed copy (half on), drawing to name (full on) — and the shapes are the control bulb with no meaning at all.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “They made patients draw the same objects three ways: copying with the picture in front of them, copying after it was taken away for 10 seconds, and drawing from just the name. Each step needs more knowledge. They also had them copy meaningless shapes after a delay — if that was fine, then the problem really is meaning, not memory for pictures.”
By grading how much each task relies on meaning — and adding a meaningless control — Study 2 can attribute drawing errors to semantic memory rather than to perception or episodic memory.

🧩 Comprehension Check

1. Why were prompts “non-perceptual”?

So they wouldn’t give away visual features (beak, wings). Otherwise the drawing would reflect the prompt rather than the patient’s own knowledge.

2. What does delayed copying recruit that immediate copying does not?

Visual episodic memory and semantic memory. The geometric-shape condition isolates the episodic part, because shapes carry almost no meaning.

3. Why was a 10-second delay chosen?

Pilot testing with DS (delays from 10 s to 1 min) showed that already after 10 s his poor conceptual knowledge began to influence his copies.

Lecture 9 of 11 · Study 2 · Results (1)

📊Study 2: task, severity and domain

Every prediction from the design came true — plus one puzzle about animals.

  • 🎯Task: immediate copy best, delayed copy worse, drawing to name worst — exactly tracking the reliance on conceptual knowledge.
  • 📈Severity: DC made more errors than DS, IF more than DC (all p < .001).
  • ✖️Interaction: the gaps between tasks widen with semantic impairment — immediate copying barely changes, the other two collapse.
  • 🕳️Omissions: effects of patient and task, but no effect of domain (F(1, 433) = 1.63, n.s.).
  • ➕Intrusions: effects of patient, task and domain — more intrusions for animals (F(1, 433) = 55.6, p < .001), mainly in delayed copy and drawing to name.
🪝 Memory hook: immediate copying is a flat line, the other two are staircases — the more a task needs meaning, the steeper the stairs as the disease advances.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “Copying with the picture in view stayed almost perfect, even in the worst patient. But copying from memory, and especially drawing from a name, got much worse as the disease got worse. Animals also got more wrong features added than objects did — which needs explaining.”
Drawing accuracy depends on how much a task relies on conceptual knowledge and on how degraded that knowledge is — not on the ability to see or draw.

Lecture 10 of 11 · Study 2 · Results (2)

🦆Distinctiveness solves the puzzle

Distinctive features go first; domain-wide features stay and spill over. Add that to the analysis and the “animal effect” vanishes.

  • 🕳️Omissions: distinctive > shared-by-category > shared-by-domain (F(2, 222) = 186.23, p < .001). Distinctive vs shared: F = 311.5; category vs domain: F = 53.4.
  • ➕Intrusions: shared-by-domain > shared-by-category > distinctive (F(2, 32) = 19.12, p < .001) — intruders come from the domain’s common pool.
  • 📶The distinctiveness effect is small in immediate copying and large in the two memory-based conditions; it is strongest in IF (most severe).
  • 🐾The animal > artefact intrusion effect disappears once distinctiveness is included: one common cause in both domains.
  • 🔺Geometric shapes: patients within the control range; only at the low end for the most complex shapes (16.7 vs 22.0).
🪝 Memory hook: the duck’s first name (bill, webbed feet) is forgotten; its family name (“animal”: legs, eyes) survives — and gets written on the wrong family member.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “The features patients leave out are the special ones that make an object unique. The features they wrongly add are the ones almost every animal has, like four legs. Animals just have more of those common features than objects do, which is why animals seemed worse — it isn’t a special ‘animal’ problem.”
Omissions and intrusions are two sides of one coin: distinctive features are lost first, and domain-wide shared features intrude — which fully explains the apparent animal–artefact difference.

1. Which features are most often omitted, and which most often intrude?

Distinctive features are omitted most; features shared across the whole domain intrude most.

2. Explain why the domain effect on intrusions disappeared.

Animals have more shared features (0.59 vs 0.29 per item). Since shared features are what intrude, animals show more intrusions. Once distinctiveness is entered as a predictor, domain adds nothing: both domains follow the same rule.

3. What does the geometric-shape result rule out?

An episodic (visual) memory explanation of poor delayed copying: with meaningless shapes, patients performed within the control range.

Lecture 11 of 11 · General discussion

🏛️What SD teaches about semantic memory

The authors’ conclusion, the alternative they cannot rule out, and why they prefer theirs.

  • 🌐Unitary amodal system: SD reflects degradation of one semantic system — “unitary (but very distributed)” — that every task draws on.
  • 🔗Consistency of performance: drawing, naming and matching correlate highly; non-semantic tasks (immediate copy, shapes, Rey) are spared → a common cause.
  • 🔁Consistency of errors: omitting specific properties while keeping domain-wide ones is also seen in naming, definitions and sorting; four-legged ducks parallel over-extended names of typical items (Hodges et al., 1995).
  • ⚖️Rival view: a separate structural description system (Riddoch et al., 1988). Not refuted — but the authors prefer an interactive account.
  • ✏️Drawing as assessment: no method is superior — drawing says nothing about use or location, but shows fine gradations and, with delayed copying, needs no verbal input or output.
🪝 Memory hook: the four-legged duck and the patient who calls every animal by one familiar name are the same error in two languages — pictures and words — because they come from one store.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “All the evidence points to one big, spread-out knowledge network that naming, understanding and drawing all rely on. When it degrades, the details go and the generic stuff remains — in words and in pictures alike. Some people think there’s a separate store for what things look like, and the authors can’t fully rule that out, but it wouldn’t explain why drawing tracks naming so closely.”
SD arises from degradation of a unitary, amodal but highly distributed conceptual network, in which visual knowledge depends on — and fails with — semantic knowledge.

🧩 Comprehension Check

1. Exam-style: what can we learn from semantic dementia about semantic memory?

(1) Semantic memory is a unitary, amodal system: drawing, naming and matching decline together (r = .96–.99). (2) It dissociates from perception, praxis and episodic memory: copying and geometric-shape recall are normal. (3) Concepts are distributed feature patterns: distinctive features are lost first and domain-shared features intrude (the four-legged duck), so the hierarchy seen in SD emerges from feature overlap. (4) Apparent category-specific effects can arise from feature distribution.

2. What is the rival account, and why do the authors reject it?

That visual/structural knowledge sits in an independent structural description system, damaged in addition to semantics. It is not refuted, but it can’t explain why drawing correlates so strongly with verbal tasks or why distinctive visual features are especially vulnerable. The authors prefer an interactive account in which visual representations depend on semantic feedback.
